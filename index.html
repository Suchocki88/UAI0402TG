📋 Lesson Overview

🎯 The Big Idea

Students discover that AI learns prejudices and assumptions directly from training data created by humans. This lesson bridges human bias (Lesson 4.1) to AI bias, showing how biases get "baked into" systems through the examples AI learns from. The key insight: AI doesn't create new biases—it amplifies and perpetuates human biases present in training data.

Why This Lesson Matters

This is the crucial bridge between understanding human cognition (4.1) and investigating AI systems (4.3+). Students must grasp that AI bias isn't a mysterious technical failure—it's a direct consequence of training on human-created data. The phrase "garbage in, garbage out" applies not just to factual accuracy but to cultural assumptions, stereotypes, and prejudices.

✅ Success Indicators

  • Students can explain how training data transfers human biases to AI systems
  • Students can identify specific examples of AI bias (image recognition, translation, autocomplete)
  • Students connect Chapter 2's training data concepts to bias implications
  • Students understand that AI amplifies existing biases rather than creating new ones
  • Students apply "garbage in, garbage out" to cultural assumptions, not just factual errors

Lesson Connections

  • Chapter 2: Training data determines AI capabilities—now students see it also determines AI values and biases
  • Lesson 4.1: Human cognitive biases exist in all of us—including people who create training data

🎯 Learning Objectives

Content Knowledge

  • Students will explain how human biases in training data transfer to AI systems
  • Students will identify three real-world examples of AI bias (image recognition, translation, autocomplete)
  • Students will articulate why AI amplifies rather than creates biases
  • Students will apply "garbage in, garbage out" to cultural assumptions

Skills & Practices

  • Students will analyze how specific training data characteristics lead to specific biases
  • Students will connect abstract concepts (bias in data) to concrete outcomes (biased AI behavior)
  • Students will evaluate claims about AI "objectivity" using evidence

Dispositions & Mindsets

  • Students will approach AI bias as a solvable problem, not a reason for cynicism
  • Students will recognize that understanding bias sources enables better AI use
  • Students will maintain curiosity about AI systems rather than blind trust or rejection

⚠️ Critical Preparation

This lesson can trigger two problematic reactions:

  • Defensiveness: "I'm not biased, so my data wouldn't be biased!"
  • Cynicism: "So AI is just useless and unfair?"

Prepare to redirect both: Understanding bias sources helps us work WITH AI more effectively, not abandon it. Awareness is power, not paralysis.

Room Setup

Standard classroom arrangement works well. Ensure all students can see projected case study examples clearly. Consider groupings for the "training data detective" activity.

⏱️ Lesson Flow (50 minutes)

0:00 - 0:05
(5 min)

Opening: Bridge from Yesterday

  • Quick recall: "Yesterday we learned that everyone has cognitive biases. Why do they exist?"
  • Bridge question: "Now imagine thousands of people—all with their own biases—creating the examples AI learns from. What might happen?"
  • Frame today's focus: "Today we discover how human biases become AI biases"
Tip: Reference the Chapter 2 knock-knock joke training. "Remember how the quality of training jokes affected the AI's jokes? The same principle applies to biases."
0:05 - 0:20
(15 min)

Part I: Case Studies of AI Bias

  • Case 1: Image Recognition (5 min)
    • Present: AI systems performing worse on darker skin tones
    • Ask: "What training data problem could cause this?"
    • Reveal: Training datasets overrepresented lighter-skinned faces
  • Case 2: Translation (5 min)
    • Present: "The doctor... he" vs. "The nurse... she" defaults
    • Ask: "Where did the AI learn these associations?"
    • Reveal: Training text contained gendered professional patterns
  • Case 3: Autocomplete (5 min)
    • Present: Search suggestions that reflect stereotypes
    • Ask: "Who created the searches the AI learned from?"
    • Reveal: AI learned patterns from millions of biased human searches
Note: For each case, follow the pattern: Present the bias → Ask students to hypothesize the training data cause → Confirm the connection. Students should discover the pattern, not just hear it.
0:20 - 0:30
(10 min)

Part II: The Core Insight

  • Draw diagram on board: Human Bias → Training Data → AI Learning → Biased Output
  • Introduce key phrase: "Garbage in, garbage out"
  • Expand the concept: This applies to cultural assumptions, not just factual errors
  • Key insight discussion: "AI doesn't CREATE new biases—it AMPLIFIES existing ones"
  • Why amplification matters: AI operates at massive scale, affecting millions of decisions
Key Point: Make explicit that AI can make bias WORSE by operating at scale and appearing "objective." When a human is biased, it affects their decisions. When AI is biased, it affects millions of decisions—and people often don't question it because "the computer said so."
0:30 - 0:38
(8 min)

Part III: Training Data Detective

  • Present new scenarios; students predict training data problems:
    • "An AI writing tool suggests mostly male names for CEO characters"
    • "A voice assistant struggles to understand accented English"
    • "A loan approval AI rejects applications from certain zip codes"
  • Students work in pairs to hypothesize: What was probably in (or missing from) the training data?
  • Quick share-out: 2-3 pairs explain their reasoning
0:38 - 0:45
(7 min)

Field Journal Reflection

  • Students complete reflection prompts individually
  • Prompts focus on: connecting to Chapter 2, applying "garbage in, garbage out," and implications for AI use
  • Circulate to ensure engagement
0:45 - 0:50
(5 min)

Closing: So What Do We Do?

  • Address potential cynicism: "Understanding this makes us BETTER at using AI, not worse"
  • Preview: "Tomorrow we become bias detectives—hunting for hidden assumptions in Alia"
  • Empowerment message: "Now you know WHERE bias comes from. That's the first step to recognizing it."

👨‍🏫 Facilitation Guide

Opening Bridge Script

SAY
"Yesterday we discovered something important: everyone has biases. They're cognitive shortcuts our brains use to process information quickly. Not character flaws—just how human brains work.

Now here's today's question: Who creates the examples that AI learns from? [Pause for responses] That's right—humans. Humans with biases. So what happens when AI learns from millions of examples created by humans who all have their own biases?

Today we're going to see exactly how human biases become AI biases."

Case Study Facilitation

PATTERN FOR EACH CASE
1. Present the bias: "Here's what researchers discovered..."
2. Pause and ask: "What training data problem could cause this? Talk to your neighbor for 30 seconds."
3. Harvest responses: Take 2-3 guesses from students.
4. Confirm and explain: "Exactly. The training data [specific problem]..."
5. Connect: "So the AI didn't decide to be biased. It learned patterns from biased data."

Core Insight Script

SAY
"Let's name what we're seeing. [Draw on board: Human Bias → Training Data → AI → Biased Output]

In Chapter 2, you learned 'garbage in, garbage out'—if you train AI on bad knock-knock jokes, you get bad knock-knock jokes. The same principle applies here, but it's not just about quality. It's about VALUES.

If training data contains stereotypes about gender and profession, AI learns those stereotypes. If training data underrepresents certain groups, AI struggles with those groups. If training data reflects the perspectives of one culture, AI centers that culture.

Here's the crucial insight: AI doesn't CREATE new biases. It AMPLIFIES existing human biases—and it does so at massive scale. A biased human makes maybe hundreds of decisions. A biased AI makes MILLIONS."

Addressing Cynicism Script

SAY
"So should we just stop using AI? No! Understanding where bias comes from is the first step to doing something about it.

Think of it like learning about germs. Once people understood that germs caused disease, they could wash hands, sterilize equipment, develop treatments. They didn't stop practicing medicine—they got BETTER at it.

Same with AI. Now that you understand bias comes from training data, you can ask better questions: What data was this trained on? Whose perspectives are represented? What might be missing? Tomorrow, you'll practice being bias detectives yourselves."

Check for Understanding (End of Lesson)

Question 1

A company trains an image recognition AI using photos primarily from fashion magazines. When deployed, the AI performs poorly at identifying people with darker skin tones. Why does this happen?

A) The AI developed its own preferences about which faces to recognize
B) The training data contained mostly lighter-skinned faces, so the AI learned those patterns better
C) Image recognition technology doesn't work well with darker colors
D) The AI needs more processing power to recognize diverse faces

Answer: B – The training data lacked diversity, so the AI learned patterns primarily from lighter-skinned faces

Question 2

When someone types "The doctor picked up his..." into a search engine, the autocomplete suggests "stethoscope." When they type "The nurse picked up her..." it also suggests "clipboard." What does this pattern reveal?

A) Doctors use stethoscopes more often than nurses do
B) The AI is making accurate predictions based on real-world data
C) The training data contained gendered assumptions about professions that the AI learned and reproduces
D) The AI was programmed by developers to associate gender with specific jobs

Answer: C – The AI learned gendered assumptions about professions from patterns in its training data

Question 3

Which statement best describes the relationship between human bias and AI bias?

A) AI systems are objective and neutral, so they help eliminate human biases
B) AI creates entirely new biases that don't exist in human thinking
C) AI learns and amplifies biases that already exist in its training data
D) AI bias only occurs when developers intentionally program prejudice into systems

Answer: C – AI doesn't create new biases; it learns and amplifies biases present in human-created training data

Short Answer 1

In Chapter 2, you learned about training AI with knock-knock jokes—the quality of jokes in the training data affected the quality of jokes the AI could produce. Explain how this same principle applies to bias. Use the phrase "garbage in, garbage out" in your response.

Sample response: Just like low-quality knock-knock jokes in training data produced an AI that generated bad jokes, biased training data produces biased AI. "Garbage in, garbage out" means that if the training data contains stereotypes, prejudices, or underrepresentation of certain groups, the AI will learn and reproduce those same problems. The AI can only be as fair and accurate as the data it learned from.

Short Answer 2

Some people argue that because AI is "just math," it must be objective and fair. Based on what you learned today, explain why this argument is flawed. Give one specific example to support your explanation.

Sample response: While AI uses mathematical processes, the training data it learns from is created by humans—and humans have biases. For example, if a translation AI is trained on text where "doctor" is usually paired with "he" and "nurse" with "she," the AI will learn these gendered patterns and reproduce them, even though the math itself is neutral. The math processes the data, but the data reflects human assumptions.

♿ Differentiation & Support

For Students Who Need More Support

  • Provide a graphic organizer showing the flow: Human Bias → Training Data → AI → Output
  • Use simpler case studies with very clear cause-effect relationships
  • Offer sentence starters: "The AI learned this bias because the training data..."
  • Pair with supportive peers during Training Data Detective activity

For Advanced Learners

  • Ask them to research additional AI bias cases (facial recognition in law enforcement, resume screening)
  • Challenge: "How might you fix the training data to reduce bias? What challenges would you face?"
  • Explore: "Why might fixing training data be harder than it sounds?"
  • Connect to Lesson 3.7: How does RLHF worker bias connect to today's lesson?

For English Language Learners

  • Pre-teach: amplify, perpetuate, training data, representation
  • Provide case studies in written form with key terms highlighted
  • Use visual diagrams heavily (training data → bias flow)
  • Allow discussion in home language during pair work

For Students from Underrepresented Groups

Some students may have personally experienced the effects of AI bias (or human bias that feeds into AI). This lesson validates those experiences.

  • Acknowledge: "Some of you may have experienced something like this firsthand"
  • Validate: These aren't just abstract examples—they affect real people
  • Empower: Understanding the mechanism is the first step toward change
  • Don't put students on the spot to share personal experiences

🚧 Anticipating Challenges

Challenge: "I'm not biased, so data I create wouldn't be biased!"

Response: "Yesterday we learned that everyone has biases—they're cognitive shortcuts, not character flaws. The question isn't whether we have biases, but whether we're aware of them. Even well-intentioned people create data that reflects their perspective. That's not a criticism—it's just how human cognition works."

Challenge: "So AI is just useless and unfair?"

Response: "Understanding where bias comes from doesn't mean we give up—it means we get smarter. Doctors didn't stop treating patients when they learned about germs; they washed their hands and developed better practices. Same with AI. Now that you know bias comes from training data, you can ask better questions and make more informed decisions about when to trust AI."

Challenge: "But the AI is just reflecting reality—doctors ARE mostly men!"

Response: "That's an important point to think through. There's a difference between describing current patterns and reinforcing them. If AI always suggests male doctors, does it just reflect reality or does it also shape what people expect? And here's another question: should AI predict what IS or what SHOULD be? We'll explore this more as we continue."

Challenge: Students fixate on one case study, missing the broader pattern

Response: After discussing multiple cases, explicitly name the pattern: "Notice what all three examples have in common—the AI didn't decide to be biased. In every case, it learned patterns from data that was created by humans with biases. This is the mechanism, and it applies everywhere AI learns from human-created examples."

Challenge: Students want to discuss "fixing" AI bias in depth

Response: "That's a great question, and we'll explore it more in upcoming lessons. For now, know that understanding where bias comes from is the essential first step. You can't fix something if you don't know what's causing it. Today is about diagnosis; solutions come later."
